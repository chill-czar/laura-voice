The three terms you should be able to define cleanly

Speech encoder

Converts a waveform into a representation a model can reason over — typically by framing the audio into short windows and producing a sequence of vectors (or discrete "audio tokens" via a learned codebook). Conceptually the audio analogue of tokenising text. Crucially it can retain acoustic detail that a transcript throws away.

Vocoder / audio decoder

The inverse: turns a predicted intermediate representation (mel-spectrogram, or discrete audio codes) back into an actual waveform you can play. It's the component that makes generated speech sound like speech rather than a spectrogram. In a classic TTS stack there's an acoustic model then a vocoder; modern systems often collapse these.

Acoustic vs semantic information

Semantic = what was said (survives transcription). Acoustic = how it was said: pitch, energy, rate, timbre, hesitation (does not survive transcription). Speech-to-speech keeps both in the same representation; cascaded deliberately discards the acoustic half at the STT boundary.

For a legal intake product specifically, the transcript isn't a debugging convenience — it's part of the deliverable. The firm needs a record of what the caller said. That alone pushes me toward cascaded, and the auditability and per-stage swappability are what let me evaluate and improve it. I'd revisit speech-to-speech where emotional nuance matters more than the paper trail.

What each layer costs you (honest latency framing)

A cascaded turn is additive by nature: endpointing + STT final + LLM TTFT + TTS TTFB. In this project each of those is measured separately (see 08-latency.html), which is precisely the advantage — I can point at which stage is slow rather than guessing at one opaque number.

The mitigations that actually matter are streaming at every boundary and never waiting for a complete artifact before starting the next stage. That's why TTS begins on the first sentence rather than the full response.

If asked "what's inside GPT-4o voice / Gemini Live?" — the correct answer is: publicly they're described as natively multimodal models handling audio in and out without a text bottleneck; the exact encoder, tokenisation scheme, and decoder are not public, and I'm not going to invent detail I can't source. What I can discuss is the general architecture class and the engineering tradeoffs it implies.
